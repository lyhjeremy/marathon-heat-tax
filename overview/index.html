The Heat Tax
Boston Marathon results and race-day weather, 1940 to 2019

80 races, 614,738 finish times, one course

Above 55°F, each degree costs about 0.4% of your time

I lined up every Boston Marathon from 1940 to 2019 against the weather on the course that day. Below about 55°F the temperature makes no measurable difference. Above it, the median finisher loses 0.42% of their time per °F, which is 0.95 minutes at a 3:47 finish. The share is similar from the front of the race to the back, so slower runners lose more minutes.

Start: the question

How many minutes is "it was hot"?

The first version of this page, in June 2026, used fourteen Boston Marathons (2001 to 2014) and found that the median finish rose 1.03 minutes per °F (r = 0.86). That was a straight line through thirteen points, with gun times in some years and chip times in others, and nothing to separate the weather from a field that was growing and changing.

This version, from October 2026, uses 80 races, weather from a reanalysis at three points on the course, wind along the course, and a long-run trend for the field. It holds back the five most recent races (2015 to 2019), fits on the rest, and then checks whether the model predicts those five. It also ships the model, so you can price a race day from a live forecast.

Superseded, 28 October 2026: the June headline of 1.03 minutes per °F and "+31 minutes from 50°F to 80°F". The rate for the median runner is now 0.95 minutes per °F above 55°F, with no cost below it, so 50°F to 80°F is about 25 minutes. The section after the forecast tool explains why the number moved.

5 km: the data

Eighty races, three ways to measure a field

Results come from adrian3/Boston-Marathon-Data-Project, one file per year from 1897 to 2019. The early files are short: before 1975 they list only the leading finishers (25 to 285 a year), and from 1975 to 1995 each list stops at a time limit (3:30 in 1976, 4:00 for most of the 1980s, about 5:00 in the early 1990s). A percentile of a list cut at a time limit moves with the cut, and on a hot day more runners fall past it. So the study uses three kinds of series, each only where the list supports it:

  • Front of the race: the times of the 10th and 25th man, 1940 to 2019. Every year lists at least 25 men.
  • Sub-elite: the 100th and 1000th man, 1975 to 2019.
  • The whole field: the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentile of all finishers, in years whose list runs well past five hours (at least 2% of it slower than 5:00). That is 1996 and 1998 to 2019. 1996 is left out as the centennial race, a one-off field of about 36,000, and 2013 because the race was stopped after the bombing.

The repo's original source, llimllib/bostonmarathon, covers 2001 to 2014. The two agree on finisher counts within 0.6% in every year but 2013. Their medians match to 0.05 minutes in 2006 to 2011 and 2014. In 2001 to 2005 and 2012 adrian3's median is 4.8 to 10.2 minutes faster, because llimllib's official column in those years is gun time; adrian3 matches llimllib's net column there to within 0.2 minutes. This version uses adrian3 for every year. Wheelchair and handcycle entrants are excluded (a W bib in llimllib; a two-hour floor catches any left in adrian3's 1993 and 1995 files).

Weather is the Open-Meteo historical archive (ERA5 reanalysis, available from 1940), hourly, at the Hopkinton start, Wellesley near halfway and the Boylston Street finish. The hours come from a race table in tables/race_days.csv: April 19 through 1968 (the 20th when the 19th was a Sunday), the third Monday of April since 1969, a noon start through 2006 and 10:00 from 2007 with waves to 11:15. The front series use the first two hours after the start; the others use the start to about four hours after the last wave.

10 km: eighty years in one chart

The slow years are the hot years, in every era

Each point is one race: how far that race's time sat above or below the field's long-run trend, against the temperature on the course. The dashed line is the fitted model, flat to 55°F and rising after. 1976, at 87°F, is the hottest race in the data; 2012 (81°F) and 2004 (77°F) come next.

15 km: the confounder

The field changed more than the weather did

Over eighty years the 25th man got 35 minutes faster (2:53:32 in 1940, 2:18:01 in 2019) and the field went from 55 listed finishers to more than 26,000, about 45% of them women. Any slope against temperature has to get past that. I fitted each series five ways: with no trend, a straight-line trend, era steps plus a line, a line plus field size and the share of women, and a smooth spline in the year (a restricted cubic spline with three to five knots). The spline is the main model.

At the front, ignoring the trend halves the slope (0.24 against 0.41 for the 25th man), because hot races were not spread evenly across the decades. For the whole-field percentiles the choice barely matters: the median sits between 0.42 and 0.45 under every choice. Eras do not apply there, since 1998 to 2014 has only one standards change (2013) and one race after it.

21 km: temperature, dew point or heat index

Dry-bulb temperature wins

Humid heat is supposed to be worse, so I expected dew point or a heat index to beat the thermometer. Each candidate was scored by leave-one-year-out cross-validation inside the training years, across all nine series, as the share of the trend-only model's error it removed.

Weather termShapeError removed

Temperature with a bend at 55°F removed 37% of the error; a straight line in temperature removed 32%; the NWS heat index did about as well as temperature but not better. Dew point alone removed nothing. Added on top of temperature, every extra term (wind, dew point, humidity, rain, sun, cloud) made the cross-validated predictions worse. Boston's race days are mostly dry: the median race-day dew point is 37°F, and among the 16 whole-field training races only 2002, 2004 and 2012 went above 50°F. With so few humid races the data cannot separate humidity from heat.

25 km: who pays

The same share, so more minutes at the back

Fitted separately for nine positions in the field, the heat slope in percent sits between 0.29% and 0.46% per °F. Converted to minutes it climbs from 0.43 per °F for the 10th man (2:13) to 0.95 at the median (3:47) and 1.34 at the 90th percentile (5:01). Switch the chart to minutes to see it.

30 km: the wind

A tailwind helps the front; the field shows nothing clear

The course heads east-northeast (a bearing of about 70°), so a west or southwest wind pushes runners to Boston. The chart shows the wind component along the course on every race day. 1994 and 2011 had tailwinds near 12 mph; 2018 had a 15 mph headwind with cold rain.

For the 10th and 25th man, each mph of tailwind took 0.08% and 0.11% off the time (90% intervals 0.02 to 0.16 and 0.02 to 0.21). A 10 mph headwind costs a 2:20 runner about 1.3 minutes. For the whole-field percentiles the estimate came out with the wrong sign and an interval wide enough to include either answer, so the forecast tool applies the front-of-race rate, 0.095% per mph of headwind, to everyone and says so. Wind did not improve the cross-validated fit, so the main model leaves it out.

35 km: the holdout

Five races the model never saw

2015 to 2019 were left out of every fit and every choice above. The level of the field kept drifting after 2014, so the fairest test is the weather part alone: does the model rank those five races in the right order and by the right amounts? The chart compares each race's predicted and actual time against the five-year average.

Across the nine series and five years, the weather model's average error was 2.26% of finish time. Predicting every holdout race at the five-year average, with no weather at all, gave 2.15%. The model did well on cold, wet 2015 (0.90% against 2.76%) and warm 2016 (1.20% against 1.59%). It over-predicted 2017, a 69°F day with an 11 mph tailwind. It missed 2018 badly: 44°F, cold rain and a 15 mph headwind, a race the model scores as ideal, and the 10th man ran 7% slower than the five-year average. 33 of the 45 actual values fell inside the 80% bands (73%); in 2018 only one of nine did.

Holding the 2014 trend flat and predicting absolute times, the median finish was off by 6.3 minutes on average over the five races. The old June line, applied the same way, was off by 8.8. Both miss partly because the slower half of the field got faster after 2014: the 90th percentile in 2015 to 2017 was 6% to 7% below the 2014 trend, a year when the field was unusually large (31,809 finishers).

So the model prices heat. It has no term for cold rain, and on the races it never saw it did not beat a flat guess overall. Read the forecast below as the heat cost on a dry day, with the interval it shows.

40 km: your race

Price your own race day

Enter the time you would run on a cool, still day. The tool asks Open-Meteo for the hourly forecast on race day, averages the race hours, and applies the model for your position in the field. More than 16 days out it falls back to climatology and says so. The next Boston Marathon is the default.

    40.5 km: the June number

    Why 1.03 became 0.95, with no cost below 55°F

    Three things moved the June result, and tables/old_vs_new.csv keeps each step. First, the timing. llimllib's official column is gun time in 2001 to 2005 and 2012, and gun time adds the minutes spent reaching the start line, which grows with the field. That gap was 10.2 minutes at the median in hot 2004 and 4.8 minutes in hot 2012, so it made the hot years look slower than they were. Re-running the June fit on the same 13 races with chip times and the course-wide weather window gives 0.76 minutes per °F, not 1.03.

    Second, the shape. A straight line spreads the heat cost over every degree, including the cool ones, where the 80 races show no effect. With the bend at 55°F, the slope above it is steeper than the straight line's, and the slope below it is zero. Third, the field: the new median series starts in 1998 instead of 2001 and has a trend in the year, so a slow year early on is not read as a hot one.

    The net result for the median runner is 0.95 minutes per °F above 55°F. A 50°F race and an 80°F race are now about 25 minutes apart rather than 31, and a 50°F race and a 55°F race are the same.

    41 km: limitations

    What this doesn't show

    The data describes Boston fields in April. A runner who trained through a hot summer, or a race in a humid place, may not follow it. The weather is a reanalysis on a 0.25-degree grid (about 20 by 28 km here), not a thermometer on the road, and one average over six hours hides a cool start and a warm finish.

    The field percentiles rest on 16 races, only two of them above 70°F, so their intervals are wide and the 55°F bend was chosen across all nine series rather than for each. The bootstrap does not redo that choice, so the intervals are a little too narrow. Times before 2001 are as adrian3 publishes them; for the 2001 to 2005 and 2012 races they are chip times, and earlier ones are probably gun times.

    Field size, qualifying standards, charity places and who chose not to start on a hot morning all move the numbers, and the trend only soaks up the slow part of that. In 2012 the B.A.A. let entrants defer to 2013 because of the heat forecast, so the 2012 field was not the field that entered.

    Finish: how it was built

    Where the numbers come from

    fetch_data.py downloads the results (adrian3 pinned to commit cc4fdd8, plus llimllib for the cross-check) and the weather into a cache outside the repo. build_dataset.py writes the per-year tables, model_fit.py picks the weather terms, fits every series, runs the bootstrap and the holdout and writes model/heat_model.json. heat_model.py loads that file and is what Marathon Strategy RAG calls; the forecast tool on this page runs the same arithmetic in JavaScript. legacy_2001_2014.py rebuilds the June result.

    Results
    Boston Marathon finish times 1940 to 2019 from adrian3/Boston-Marathon-Data-Project; 2001 to 2014 checked against llimllib/bostonmarathon
    Weather
    Open-Meteo historical archive (ERA5), hourly, three course points, race hours from tables/race_days.csv
    Model
    log time = spline trend in year + slope × max(temperature − 55°F, 0), one fit per series, OLS
    Uncertainty
    1,000 bootstrap resamples of race years; 90% intervals
    Holdout
    2015 to 2019, never used for fitting or choices
    Stack
    Python, pandas, NumPy; the charts and the forecast tool are plain SVG and JavaScript

The Heat Tax
Boston Marathon results and race-day weather, 2001 to 2014

14 races, 280,459 finishers, one course

Each degree of heat costs about a minute

I paired fourteen Boston Marathons with the temperature on the course that day. The field's median finish time rises by about 1.0 minute for every °F (r = 0.86, with the interrupted 2013 race left out of the fit). Going from a 50°F race to an 80°F race moves the median finish by about 31 minutes.

Start: the question

How many minutes is "it was hot"?

Runners use heat to explain a slow day, and the explanation almost never comes with a number. I wanted the price per degree for an ordinary finisher, so I used one race run many times.

Boston has the same course every April, with a qualifying field of broadly similar runners, so a change in the median finish from one year to the next is mostly weather. Each year becomes one point: the median of between 13,422 and 31,939 finishers, set against that day's temperature.

10 km: the scale

Fourteen race days on one thermometer

Twelve of the fourteen races fell between 45°F and 64°F. Leaving out the halted 2013 race, their medians sit between 3:44 and 4:03. The two races above 70°F are the two slowest fields in the set.

21 km: the fit

A straight line through thirteen races

0.86

Pearson r, race-day temperature against field median, 2013 excluded.

An ordinary least squares fit of median finish on temperature gives a slope of 1.03 minutes per °F (standard error 0.19, p = 0.0002). Over the 30-degree range from 50°F to 80°F that is 31 minutes on the median finish.

30 km: year by year

The slow years are the hot years

In date order the same data shows two spikes, 2004 and 2012. There is no steady drift across the period. The years either side of each spike are back near 3:45 to 3:50.

35 km: limitations

What this doesn't show

Thirteen points make a descriptive relationship, not an experiment. Field size grew from 13,422 finishers in 2001 to 31,939 in 2014, and qualifying standards changed during the period. Either could move the median on its own.

2013 is out of the fit because the race was stopped after the bombing and many runners never finished, which truncates the field. Its median of 3:29 would pull the line for reasons unrelated to weather.

Temperature here also stands in for humidity, sun and wind, which were not modeled. Read the slope as the cost of a warm race day, not of air temperature alone. The result is a field median; it says nothing about how a given runner responds to heat.

40 km: using it

Set the goal from the forecast

The slope is most useful before the race. A forecast 15°F warmer than your training conditions implies a median-runner finish about 15 minutes slower. The same rule feeds the heat adjustment in Race Day Copilot and a knowledge card in Marathon Strategy RAG.

Finish: how it was built

Where the numbers come from

fetch_data.py downloads the results and the weather; analyze.py computes each year's median, fits the line with scipy.stats.linregress and writes the figures and tables/heat_tax.csv. Raw downloads are git-ignored.

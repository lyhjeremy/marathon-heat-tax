The Heat Tax
Boston Marathon results and race-day weather, 2001 to 2014

14 races, 280,459 finishers, one course

Each degree of heat costs about a minute

In 2006 it was 51°F on the Boston course and the middle of the field finished in 3:45. In 2012 it was 81°F and the middle of the field finished in 4:16. Across fourteen races the median finish rose 1.03 minutes for every °F (r = 0.86, with the halted 2013 race left out of the fit).

Start: the question

How many minutes is "it was hot"?

Runners blame the heat for a slow day all the time, and the excuse rarely comes with a number. I wanted a price per degree for an ordinary finisher.

Boston is a good place to look. The course is the same every April and the field is a qualifying field of broadly similar runners, so when the median moves from one year to the next, weather is the likeliest cause. Each year gives one point: the median of between 13,422 and 31,939 finishers against that day's temperature.

10 km: the scale

The slow years are the hot years

Twelve of the fourteen races fell between 45°F and 64°F, and leaving out the halted 2013 race, their medians sit between 3:44 and 4:03. The only two races above 70°F, 2004 and 2012, produced the two slowest fields. The years either side of each were back near 3:45 to 3:50.

21 km: the fit

A straight line through thirteen races

An ordinary least squares fit of median finish on temperature gives 1.03 minutes per °F (standard error 0.19, p = 0.0002). Over the 30 degrees from 50°F to 80°F that is 31 minutes. Switch the chart to the faster or slower quarter of the field, or put 2013 back into the fit, and the line is refitted in your browser from the same fourteen rows.

30 km: who pays

Heat costs slower runners more

The quartile columns in the same table answer a second question. The runner a quarter of the way down the field loses 0.76 minutes per °F; the runner three quarters of the way down loses 1.35. As a share of finish time that is 0.36% per °F against 0.51%, so the gap is not only that a longer race has more minutes to lose.

40 km: limitations

What this doesn't show

Thirteen points can describe a relationship but cannot prove a cause. Field size grew from 13,422 finishers in 2001 to 31,939 in 2014, and qualifying standards changed during the period. Either could move the median on its own.

2013 is out of the fit because the race was stopped after the bombing and many runners never finished, which truncates the field. Its median of 3:29 would pull the line for reasons unrelated to weather.

Temperature also stands in for humidity, sun and wind, none of which were modeled. The slope is the cost of a warm Boston race day with all of those folded in, measured on the field and not on any one runner.

Finish: how it was built

Where the numbers come from

fetch_data.py downloads the results and the weather. analyze.py computes each year's median and quartiles, fits the line with scipy.stats.linregress and writes the figures and tables/heat_tax.csv. Raw downloads are git-ignored. The same rule feeds the heat adjustment in Race Day Copilot and a knowledge card in Marathon Strategy RAG.
